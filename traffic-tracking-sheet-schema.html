Design a One-Page Traffic Tracking Sheet You Can Audit

A useful tracking sheet records what happened, where a number came from, and what remains unknown. It should never turn clicks into confirmed leads or imply that an attribution label proves causation.

Home / Design a One-Page Traffic Tracking Sheet You Can Audit
In brief: A useful tracking sheet records what happened, where a number came from, and what remains unknown. It should never turn clicks into confirmed leads or imply that an attribution label proves causation.

Choose the unit of a row before choosing columns

For a beginner’s weekly report, use one row per landing page, channel and calendar week. This is an aggregate reporting grain: a row summarizes a defined slice, not an individual visitor. Do not mix daily rows with weekly totals or add an overall total to the same table and then sum everything; that double-counts results. Write the property time zone and the first and last day of the week above the sheet.

For example, “week beginning 2026-10-26, landing page /traffic-to-leads-checklist.html, Organic Search” is a defined row. “Google, 150 clicks” is not enough because it lacks a period, metric source and landing-page scope. Keep a separate notes field for uncertainties such as missing campaign tags or a reporting configuration change.

Do not place email addresses, names, IP addresses, or unredacted form responses in a traffic-summary spreadsheet. Use aggregated counts. If the email provider cannot connect a signup to a landing-page channel reliably, enter “not available” for that channel’s signup count rather than assigning all signups to the last link someone clicked.

Separate source, medium and campaign from the channel grouping

A channel is a reporting classification, not the full source history. In a separate campaign log, record the utm_source, utm_medium and utm_campaign values that you intentionally attached to a shared link. Use lower-case naming consistently, avoid personally identifying values and document redirects. A link with a UTM tag is evidence of tagging, not proof that a human read the page.

For a simple campaign, choose utm_source=newsletter, utm_medium=email, and utm_campaign=october_worksheet. Do not fabricate these parameters in a report after the fact. The UTM guide explains construction, and the Direct traffic audit explains why attribution can be missing or misleading.

Worked example with fictional counts

Imagine one landing page over a seven-day period. A report shows 80 Organic Search sessions and 43 engaged sessions. The newsletter provider confirms three signups across the site, but its confirmation records do not establish which channel each person came from. Record 80 and 43 in the Organic Search row. Leave the channel-specific confirmed signup cell as not attributable; put the three provider-confirmed signups in a separate site-level total with the same date range.

The observed engagement rate for this row is 43 ÷ 80 = 53.75%. That is a descriptive ratio, not proof of content quality. A change in consent collection, bot filtering, tagging or reporting definitions can alter the number. If you had three reliably attributable signups from these 80 sessions, the observed signup rate would be 3 ÷ 80 = 3.75%; do not calculate that rate from the site-level total in this example.

This distinction matters when a dashboard displays an attractive conversion percentage without showing the attribution method. A precise-looking result can still be wrong if its numerator and denominator cover different populations.

Write formulas with explicit denominators

  1. Engagement rate: engaged sessions divided by sessions, only when both came from the same reporting scope. Return “not available” if sessions are zero.
  2. Confirmed signup rate: attributable confirmed signups divided by sessions, only when attribution is sufficiently reliable. Label it “observed,” not guaranteed.
  3. Change versus prior period: (current − previous) divided by previous, only if the definitions and date-window lengths match. If previous is zero, show “not meaningful” rather than an infinite percentage.
  4. Effort: record the hours spent producing and maintaining the content separately; hours are not an advertising expense or a financial return.

Never add Search Console clicks to GA4 sessions. They are different measurements. If you need both, place them in separately labeled columns and review the Search Console versus GA4 worksheet.

Make the sheet safe to reuse

Protect the header row, use data validation for dates and nonnegative counts, and add a note naming the analytics property and time zone. Keep raw exports separate from the summary so a later correction can be traced. When a metric definition changes, record the date in a change log and avoid comparing across the break without an explanation.

For collaboration, grant access only to people who need it. Do not publish internal campaign notes or subscriber-level data in a public spreadsheet. A copyable blank template can contain column names and fictional examples without exposing real visitors.

Review the sheet without chasing a vanity metric

Look first for data-quality problems: broken links, unexplained Direct traffic, missing page paths or an unexpected change in reporting. Then read actual visitor questions and inspect whether the page answers them. With small samples, a few extra signups can shift rates dramatically. Report uncertainty rather than announcing a winning channel. The metrics dictionary is a useful companion when two reports use the same word differently.
