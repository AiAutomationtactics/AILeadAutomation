Is Your Community Comment Helpful or Promotional? A Decision Rubric

A comment can mention a resource and still help, but a polite tone cannot rescue a reply that mainly sends people to a sales page. Use this decision rubric before posting.

Home / Is Your Community Comment Helpful or Promotional? A Decision Rubric
In brief: A comment can mention a resource and still help, but a polite tone cannot rescue a reply that mainly sends people to a sales page. Use this decision rubric before posting.

First apply four non-negotiable checks

Before scoring anything, confirm the community permits the type of participation you intend. A helpful answer in the wrong venue can still violate rules. Read the current community description, pinned moderator notices and any topic-specific restrictions. If you cannot check the rules, do not post a promotional link while you investigate.

  1. Relevance: Is the comment answering the actual question, rather than redirecting to your preferred product?
  2. Permission: Does the community allow this type of reply and, if applicable, this type of link?
  3. Truthfulness: Can you support any factual claims, and have you labeled hypothetical examples?
  4. Privacy: Are you avoiding unsolicited direct messages, scraping identities and adding members to lists without their separate consent?

A failure on permission, factual honesty or privacy is a stop condition, not a point deduction that can be compensated for with clever writing. Moderators may apply stricter standards than this worksheet. Platform rules can change, so check the actual community before posting.

The seven-part comment quality rubric

For a draft that passes the stop conditions, assign 0, 1 or 2 points to each criterion. This is an editorial aid created for this guide, not a platform-approved algorithm or a prediction of moderation decisions.

A draft scoring 12–14 may be ready for a final policy check; 9–11 usually deserves revision; 0–8 should be rewritten around the reader's problem. These bands are suggested internal editing thresholds, not claims about reach, ranking or acceptance. Even a 14-point draft should not be posted if it fails a stop condition.

Worked example: a visitor-count question

Imagine a beginner asks: “My promotion dashboard shows 150 clicks, but my analytics show 90 sessions. Did 60 visitors disappear?” The numbers here are fictional for teaching; they are not a campaign result.

Draft A: promotional

“You need better traffic. Visit my system and get thousands of visitors instantly. Message me for the link.”

This does not explain measurement differences, uses an unsupported result claim and tries to move the discussion into private messages. It fails the truthfulness check and should not be posted. Adding a disclosure at the end would not fix the core problem.

Draft B: useful and self-contained

“Those numbers may be measuring different events. A platform can count a click before your page fully loads, while site analytics applies its own session rules and may not record every visitor. Compare the same date range and timezone first. Then test the destination link on a phone, check whether analytics loads, and review any consent or blocking settings. The difference alone does not prove fraud or missing customers.”

Draft B addresses the question, gives a diagnostic sequence and explains uncertainty. It needs no referral link. A reader can act on it immediately. The answer should be adjusted if the specific analytics provider defines clicks or sessions differently.

When a resource link genuinely helps

Sometimes the answer needs a long screenshot sequence or a worksheet that would overwhelm the discussion. In that case, give the short answer first, then explain exactly what the optional resource adds. For example: “If the community permits resources, the campaign metrics dictionary has a comparison worksheet.” If the rule forbids links, keep the answer in the comment and omit the link.

When referring to your own website, say that it is yours if that relationship matters to readers. When a destination pays you for referrals, make the financial relationship obvious near the recommendation and follow the community's rules. HTML attributes such as rel="sponsored nofollow" on a website are not a substitute for an in-comment disclosure. Do not hide a prohibited referral behind a tracking redirect.

Run a two-person review without chasing engagement

For a team, ask one person to score the draft and a second person to check whether the first action is useful without a click. Compare disagreements: if one reviewer thinks the link is necessary and another does not, remove it and see whether the answer still works. Record the decision, not member identities. Avoid saving names, screenshots of private conversations or personal contact details in your review log.

After posting, respond to genuine follow-up questions and correct errors. Do not repeatedly repost a near-identical comment across groups. A moderator removal is feedback to revisit the rules, not a signal to evade moderation with a different account.
