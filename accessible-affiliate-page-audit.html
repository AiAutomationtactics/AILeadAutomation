Audit a Small Affiliate Landing Page for Accessibility and Trust

Accessibility is part of usefulness. A visitor should be able to understand your offer, use the page with a keyboard, and leave without being trapped.

Home / Audit a Small Affiliate Landing Page for Accessibility and Trust
What you will learn: A keyboard-and-screen-reader-minded review of headings, links, form labels, contrast, errors, consent and non-deceptive navigation.

Start with the visitor's task

Imagine a reader arriving at a page that offers a free email checklist. The page should explain what the checklist contains, what happens after signup, and whether promotional emails may follow. The reader should be able to navigate the page, enter an address, understand errors, and decide not to subscribe.

A visually attractive page can still fail this test. Decorative labels, ambiguous buttons, low contrast, or surprise redirects make a page harder to use. Accessibility and honest marketing reinforce each other.

Test the page without a mouse

Open the page and press Tab repeatedly. Can you reach the navigation, primary content, form fields, checkbox, submit button, and footer in a logical order? Is the focused element visibly highlighted? Can you activate links and buttons using the keyboard?

A “Get access” button should have an understandable destination. If it opens a third-party checkout, the text and surrounding context should make that clear. Do not attach hidden navigation behavior to the Back button or open unrelated pages on exit.

Check that a “Skip to content” link works. It should move keyboard users past repeated navigation to the main content.

Use a meaningful heading structure

The page needs one clear main heading describing the actual offer. Section headings should identify distinct topics, such as “What you receive,” “How signup works,” and “Privacy and unsubscribe.” Do not use headings merely because their default font size looks attractive.

A reader using a screen reader should be able to skim headings and understand the page's structure. Keep essential information as real text rather than baking it into an image.

Label every form field

Visible text labels should be programmatically associated with inputs. Placeholder text alone is insufficient because it disappears when someone starts typing. For example, use a label for “Email address” connected to the matching input ID.

The W3C form-label tutorial shows why explicit labels help both visual and assistive-technology users. Apply the same principle to checkboxes: the consent text should be easy to read and clickable.

Explain whether fields are required before submission. If an error occurs, identify the field and tell the reader how to correct it; color alone should not be the only signal.

Check contrast and mobile readability

Review text, buttons, and links against their backgrounds. A pale button label may look stylish but be hard to read. Test at increased browser zoom and on a narrow screen. The page should not require horizontal scrolling to read normal paragraphs or operate the form.

Do not make the only unsubscribe information a tiny low-contrast footer line. Consent and data-use explanations should be legible where the decision is made.

Make disclosures understandable

If a link may pay you a commission, say so in plain language near the recommendation. Do not hide that relationship in a hover tooltip. If the offer is an email signup, distinguish educational updates from possible promotional messages.

Do not imply that a free resource requires purchasing a third-party product unless that is genuinely a condition and clearly stated. Avoid fake countdown timers, invented testimonials, and ambiguous “continue” buttons.

A 20-minute audit worksheet

Spend five minutes navigating with only a keyboard, five minutes checking labels and errors, five minutes testing mobile zoom and contrast, and five minutes reading the page as a skeptical first-time visitor. Record each problem as location, impact, fix, and verification.

For example: “Signup form, checkbox text too small, consent difficult to read on mobile, increase font size and spacing, retest at 200% zoom.” A fix is complete only after it is tested again.

Accessibility is an ongoing process, not a one-time certification. The WCAG 2.2 guidance provides a broader reference for testing beyond this beginner checklist.
