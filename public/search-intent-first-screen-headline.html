Write a Landing-Page Headline That Answers Search Intent

A useful landing-page headline tells the reader what task the page helps them complete. Pair it with a first-screen explanation that shows what is available, who it is for, and what happens next—without inventing urgency or results.

In brief: A useful landing-page headline tells the reader what task the page helps them complete. Pair it with a first-screen explanation that shows what is available, who it is for, and what happens next—without inventing urgency or results.

Start with the visitor's task, not your preferred slogan

Before writing a headline, describe the entry point. A reader searching “how to track free website traffic” may want a spreadsheet schema, while someone searching “traffic tracking software comparison” may be evaluating tools. Those are different tasks even though both contain the word traffic. A page that promises a worksheet but immediately asks visitors to buy software breaks that expectation. Write a one-sentence task statement: “A beginner wants to record where visits came from and whether any opted-in subscribers resulted.” Then decide whether your page actually provides the promised help.

Search queries are only clues. The exact query may be unavailable for some visits, and a single phrase can express several intents. Review the actual page, existing reader questions and the content of any referring post before deciding. Do not infer what every visitor thinks from a keyword alone. The search-intent mapping guide explains how to separate informational, comparison and action-oriented questions.

Use a four-part first-screen contract

On a typical mobile screen, a visitor should be able to find a clear answer to four questions without hunting through decorative cards. What is this? Name the resource or service in ordinary language. Who is it for? State the relevant experience level or situation. What can I do here? Describe a specific next action. What will it cost or require? Explain when an email, payment, account or external tool is necessary. This is an editorial framework, not a claim that a certain number of words or pixels guarantees conversions.

The headline handles the main promise. The supporting paragraph clarifies scope and limits. A descriptive link or button begins the task. If the offer is free and available without registration, say so accurately. If a form is optional, do not position it as the only route to a supposedly ungated resource.

Notice that the revised headline describes a result the page can actually enable: organizing data. It does not claim to generate visitors or commissions. The supporting text names the format and inputs, while the button tells the reader what will open. A real page must contain that worksheet before using this copy. If it only contains a newsletter signup, the copy should instead say “Subscribe for the weekly traffic-tracking lesson,” with accurate frequency and consent information.

Match the headline to the actual depth of the page

“Complete SEO audit” is an overstatement for a six-item introductory checklist. “Check six common technical SEO issues before launch” is more precise. Similarly, “Compare the best affiliate tools” implies research and evidence; if the page only explains how to choose evaluation criteria, call it a comparison worksheet instead. Avoid unsupported superlatives such as best, fastest, guaranteed or proven unless the claim has relevant evidence, clear boundaries and a current source.

Use one descriptive H1 that reflects the page's actual purpose. The browser title can be a concise variation rather than an identical slogan repeated everywhere. Google may generate its own title link, so metadata is an informative signal rather than a promise of how a result will display. Keep the H1 visible and meaningful even when CSS fails. Do not hide a second keyword-stuffed headline for search engines.

Design the first screen for a narrow phone

Test a viewport around 360 CSS pixels wide, but also zoom text to 200% and use a keyboard. A sticky header, giant illustration or several promotional banners can crowd the main explanation off screen. The solution is not to make the font tiny. Prioritize a readable H1, a brief plain-language summary and one meaningful action. Place secondary navigation and disclosures where they remain discoverable without blocking the task.

Buttons should say where they lead: “Read the guide,” “Open the worksheet,” or “Subscribe to monthly updates.” Do not label an outbound affiliate referral “Download the free worksheet.” If a paid product is genuinely part of the page, clearly explain the seller, product, price and terms near the relevant action rather than disguising the purchase behind a generic button.

Run a five-person comprehension check without pretending it is a scientific test

Show a draft first screen to a few people who resemble the intended reader, without explaining it aloud. Ask each person to say what they think the page offers, whether a purchase or signup is required, and what will happen after pressing the primary button. Record the words they use, not only whether they say they “like” it. A small convenience sample is useful for finding confusing wording, but cannot establish conversion-rate improvements for all audiences.

Example: three readers expect an immediately available spreadsheet, but the button opens an email form. That is a concrete mismatch. Either make the spreadsheet accessible or rewrite the promise as an email-delivered resource and explain the subscription separately. Do not reinterpret the confusion as evidence that visitors are unqualified.

Know when to revise instead of adding another page

If an existing page already answers the same question, improve its headline and explanation rather than making a near-duplicate URL. Keep its canonical URL stable when practical and check whether internal links still point to the correct resource. Review the landing-page blueprint for page structure and the mobile speed triage for performance-related problems. A good headline cannot compensate for missing content, broken links or a form that never confirms delivery.
