Interview Potential Readers Without Turning Research into a Sales Pitch

Five honest conversations can reveal a confusing step that analytics never explain. Treat interviews as research, not as permission to market to people.

Home / Interview Potential Readers Without Turning Research into a Sales Pitch
What you will learn: A seven-question reader interview script, consent checklist and simple method for turning real observations into ethical content decisions.

The purpose is to hear a real workflow

Suppose you publish guides for people starting a newsletter. Analytics show that a page about signup forms is popular, but you do not know whether readers are confused by the form itself, consent wording, or the confirmation email. A brief conversation can identify the step that actually blocks them.

An interview is not a testimonial request, a lead capture trick, or a product pitch. Tell the participant what you want to learn, how long it should take, and whether you will keep notes. Participation should be voluntary. Do not assume that permission to answer questions is permission to receive marketing emails.

Recruit without pressure

Invite a small range of people who have encountered the relevant task, including someone who completed it and someone who stopped. Avoid relying only on enthusiastic customers or affiliate partners. State that you are improving educational material and that there is no obligation to buy anything.

Before taking notes, explain whether answers will be anonymous, who will see them, and how long you intend to retain them. Do not request passwords, account numbers, health details, or screenshots containing personal information. If you record audio, obtain explicit permission and explain the recording's purpose.

Seven open-ended questions

  1. What were you trying to accomplish when you started this task?
  2. What did you try first, and why did that seem reasonable?
  3. Where did you pause, get confused, or decide to stop?
  4. What information did you look for at that point?
  5. Which explanation or example helped, and which made things harder?
  6. How did you decide whether the task was finished?
  7. If a beginner asked you for help tomorrow, what warning would you give them?

Ask “Can you walk me through that?” when a response is vague. Do not suggest an answer such as “Was the signup button too small?” until the participant has described the problem in their own words.

Separate facts from interpretations

Use a note template with four fields: participant task, observed difficulty, exact wording if permission allows, and possible content response. The fourth field is your hypothesis, not the participant's claim.

For example, a participant might say they could not tell whether a newsletter confirmation email arrived. Your proposed response could be a troubleshooting section covering spam folders, confirmation states, and provider settings. Do not write “most beginners never receive confirmation emails” from a single conversation.

If multiple participants mention different problems, resist forcing them into one grand conclusion. A small interview set is directional evidence, not a representative survey.

Turn a finding into a testable tutorial

Choose one repeated, specific obstacle. Draft a guide that shows the expected state, the failure state, and a safe recovery step. Ask a reader unfamiliar with the draft to follow it. Record where they hesitate and improve the instructions.

A useful output from three interviews might be a one-page “Before you send traffic to your signup form” checklist. The checklist should describe observable checks: a visible label, clear consent, working submission, and a confirmation message. It should not promise more subscribers.

Avoid common research mistakes

  • Do not treat a friend or family member as a neutral sample without noting the relationship.
  • Do not publish identifying quotations without permission.
  • Do not count an unreturned invitation as a negative opinion.
  • Do not ask leading questions that validate a product you already want to promote.
  • Do not add interviewees to an autoresponder unless they separately and knowingly subscribe.

A 30-minute research session

Spend five minutes explaining the purpose and consent, fifteen minutes on the participant's actual process, five minutes clarifying details, and five minutes confirming that you understood their difficulty. Afterward, summarize one finding and one uncertainty. If the finding does not justify a new article, update an existing page.

This is a way to improve reader usefulness, not a shortcut to inventing “customer proof.” Honest limitations make the eventual content more credible.
