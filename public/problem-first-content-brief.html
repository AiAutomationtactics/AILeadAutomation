Write a Problem-First Content Brief That Produces a Useful Tutorial

A good content brief defines what the reader will accomplish and what evidence the author must supply. Keywords and calls to action come later.

Home / Write a Problem-First Content Brief That Produces a Useful Tutorial
What you will learn: A fill-in editorial brief with audience, outcome, evidence, worked example, boundaries and a publish-or-merge decision.

Begin with a concrete reader problem

A vague brief says “write a comprehensive article about affiliate traffic.” It offers no way to judge whether the article helped. A stronger brief says “help a first-time publisher distinguish visits, outbound clicks, and confirmed email signups after sharing one educational post.” The second version gives the writer a clear task, a starting skill level, and a measurable result.

Before drafting, decide whether your planned page answers a question that is not already covered on the site. Search your existing titles and read the closest article. A new URL is justified by a distinct reader outcome, not by a different wording of the same keyword.

The seven-field brief

  1. Reader and starting point: Who is doing the task, and what do they already know?
  2. One job to finish: Express the desired result as an observable action.
  3. Direct answer: Draft two sentences that could stand alone at the top of the page.
  4. Evidence: Identify sources, calculations, screenshots, or examples you can verify.
  5. Steps and obstacles: List the minimum sequence and the most likely failure.
  6. Boundaries: State what the guide cannot establish or guarantee.
  7. Next action: Offer a worksheet, relevant follow-up guide, or a neutral checklist.

A useful brief is short enough that an editor can identify missing evidence at a glance. It is not a prompt to produce thousands of words regardless of need.

Worked example: a newsletter traffic report

Reader: A blogger who posted a tutorial on two social platforms and uses an email signup form.

Outcome: Build a weekly table with campaign name, tagged visits, form submissions, and confirmed subscribers, without treating these as identical.

Direct answer: “Use consistent campaign tags and record each stage separately. Compare trends, but do not assume every confirmed subscriber can be attributed perfectly to a single social post.”

Evidence: A sample UTM URL, screenshots from the owner's own tools if available, and a fictional table clearly labeled as an illustration.

Steps: Choose a campaign name; tag the shared URL; verify the destination; record analytics visits; record provider confirmations; note differences.

Failure mode: The email platform reports a confirmed subscriber while the analytics tool reports no conversion. Explain that different measurement systems, consent settings, blocked scripts, and time windows can produce discrepancies.

Boundary: This table cannot prove that a particular post caused a purchase.

Next action: Link to the site's UTM guide for implementation, rather than repeating all of its instructions.

Design the example before the introduction

If you cannot produce a meaningful example, the brief is not ready. An example should show actual decisions: a before state, an action, and an after state. For an analytics guide, invented numbers are acceptable only if clearly marked as hypothetical and used to demonstrate arithmetic, not to imply real campaign results.

Keep one “proof box” in the brief listing each factual claim and its support. A claim such as “this tactic always doubles conversions” has no place without strong, representative evidence; replace it with a neutral description of what the tactic changes and how to test it.

A readable page outline

Lead with the answer. Explain prerequisites next. Show a worked example. Then describe common errors and how to recognize them. End with a small exercise and links to genuinely relevant material. This order usually serves a beginner better than a long promotional introduction.

Use headings that describe actions, such as “Check the confirmation status,” rather than vague headings like “The secret nobody tells you.” A descriptive heading also makes a page easier to scan with assistive technology.

The publish-or-merge gate

Ask three questions: Does the page solve a distinct task? Does it add an original worked example or clear evidence? Can a reader complete the task without clicking an affiliate link? If any answer is no, revise or merge the draft.

Before publication, verify every outbound destination and disclosure. The quality scorecard can help with the final review, but it cannot replace checking facts.
