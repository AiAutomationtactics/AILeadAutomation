SPF, DKIM and DMARC for a Permission-Based Newsletter

A subscription form is not the same as a verified sending domain. Protect readers and your sending identity before increasing newsletter volume.

Authentication and consent are different checks

Email authentication helps receiving mail servers assess whether a message was sent by a service authorized to use the sender's domain. Subscriber consent answers a different question: did the recipient actually request these messages? An authenticated unsolicited message is still unsolicited. A legitimate opt-in can also miss the inbox if the sender's technical configuration is poor.

Google's email sender guidelines explain authentication requirements, with additional rules for bulk senders. The requirements can change and depend on how mail is sent, so verify the current guidance from both the receiving mailbox provider and your sending platform.

Identify the sending domain before touching DNS

Write down the visible From address, the newsletter service that sends the messages, the domain whose DNS you can edit, and the person authorized to change it. Editing an HTML site on a hosting company's subdomain does not mean you control that company's email authentication records. A form posting to an autoresponder also does not establish that the autoresponder has authenticated a custom From domain.

Ask the sending provider which domains and DNS records it supports. Keep a record of the current configuration before making changes. Do not publish records copied from a tutorial or another customer's account: authentication values are provider-specific and may depend on your exact domain and sending setup.

SPF: authorize the correct sending services

Sender Policy Framework (SPF) lets a domain publish an authorization policy for sending systems. A service may give you a TXT record or instructions to modify an existing one. Avoid publishing multiple independent SPF policies at the same hostname. When several legitimate systems send mail, their authorized sources usually need to be represented in one valid policy under the domain owner's control.

Practical example: a newsletter uses one provider while order confirmations use another. Before changing SPF, list both senders, confirm the provider instructions, and plan how to preserve both. A record that accidentally excludes order-confirmation mail can disrupt real customers. After a change, test actual messages rather than assuming that a DNS editor's save button proves the policy works.

DKIM: verify a real signed message

DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM) allows a sending service to attach a signature that a receiving system can verify using a public key published in DNS. Providers typically supply a selector and DNS value. Do not invent a selector or copy a public key from someone else's documentation. Use the records issued for the account and domain you control.

After DNS propagation, send a message to a mailbox you own and inspect its authentication results. Check the visible From address, the signing domain and whether the message actually passed. A provider dashboard may show that a record exists while a particular outgoing message still uses the wrong identity. Test the real sending path, including any separate transactional messages, before increasing volume.

DMARC: alignment and gradual enforcement

Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance (DMARC) checks whether SPF or DKIM authentication aligns appropriately with the visible From domain. It also lets domain owners express a policy for messages that fail and receive reports. A monitoring stage can help reveal forgotten sending systems before stricter enforcement. Review report handling and retention because reports may contain operational data.

Do not jump to a strict reject policy until you understand all authorized senders. If a marketing platform or transaction system is missing from your inventory, legitimate mail could be rejected. Follow your email provider's sequence and verify alignment using test messages. The correct policy is specific to the domain, not something a generic educational article can safely generate for you.

Permission, unsubscribes and list hygiene

Maintain a record of what each person agreed to receive, when the signup occurred and how it was collected. If you send commercial electronic messages in Canada, consent, sender identification and unsubscribe requirements may apply, subject to exceptions; review the CRTC's CASL guidance. This article is general education, not legal advice.

Do not import a safelist provider's advertised audience into your own email list. A service's permission to send its own promotions is not proof that its recipients agreed to receive your future newsletter. Remove or suppress addresses according to your provider's bounce and unsubscribe rules. A hard bounce, temporary delivery problem, spam complaint and unsubscribe are distinct outcomes; report them separately instead of calling every attempted message a delivered or opened email.

A launch checklist that can be repeated

  1. Identify the From domain, DNS administrator and all sending services.
  2. Read the current provider-specific SPF, DKIM and DMARC instructions.
  3. Make authorized DNS changes without breaking other legitimate senders.
  4. Send test messages to addresses you control and inspect authentication results.
  5. Confirm signup wording, privacy notice, consent record and unsubscribe route.
  6. Document the configuration and repeat the checks when changing email providers.
  7. Monitor bounces and complaints before increasing sending volume.

If using SendSteed or another hosted autoresponder, ask it for the exact supported domain-authentication procedure. Do not claim a subscriber, delivery percentage or sale unless the relevant system confirms it. The safest list is built from people who understood and voluntarily requested the emails.
