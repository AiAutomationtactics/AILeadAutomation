Build a Campaign Metrics Dictionary Before Comparing Traffic Reports

Two dashboards can report different numbers without either being fraudulent. Define each event, its source and its time window before comparing campaigns.

Home / Build a Campaign Metrics Dictionary Before Comparing Traffic Reports
What you will learn: Define clicks, sessions, leads and confirmed outcomes in a consistent measurement sheet, with an illustrative reconciliation example.

Different systems count different events

A social platform may report link clicks, your website analytics may report sessions, and your email service may report confirmed subscribers. Those numbers are not interchangeable. A click can occur without a page loading; a session can contain several actions; a form submission can remain unconfirmed.

Before deciding whether a campaign “worked,” create a small dictionary that says exactly what each number means and where it came from. This prevents a common reporting error: treating every larger number as progress toward a sale.

Six useful definitions

  • Impression: A platform-specific display event. Different services have different counting rules.
  • Outbound click: A recorded attempt to follow a link away from a source. It does not prove the destination loaded.
  • Website session: A visit grouped according to the analytics product's rules and settings.
  • Form submission: An attempt or successful delivery of information to a form endpoint, depending on the system.
  • Confirmed subscriber: A person the email provider currently considers subscribed after its configured process.
  • Attributed sale: A purchase credited to a campaign by a particular tracking or affiliate system, subject to its attribution rules.

For each metric, document the provider, report name, time zone, date range, filters, and whether consent or browser settings can affect collection.

A hypothetical reconciliation example

Assume a platform reports 120 outbound clicks during one week. Your website analytics records 93 sessions attributed to that campaign. The form provider reports 8 submissions, and 5 confirmed subscribers.

These are invented numbers for arithmetic practice, not results from an actual campaign. You should not report “120 visitors” or “8 leads acquired” without defining the terms. A useful summary is: “The platform counted 120 outbound clicks; analytics attributed 93 sessions; the form provider recorded 8 submissions and 5 confirmed subscribers.”

You may calculate 5 ÷ 93 ≈ 5.4% as a *confirmed subscribers per attributed session* ratio for this hypothetical dataset. That is not a universal conversion benchmark, nor proof that the platform caused all five signups.

Keep a small data dictionary

Use one row per metric with these fields: metric name, plain-language definition, source system, report location, time window, deduplication rule if known, and known limitations.

Example row: “Confirmed subscribers | contacts marked subscribed by email provider | list report | weekly period in local time | provider-defined | excludes unconfirmed signups; may include people from several sources.”

Do not guess how a third-party service deduplicates clicks or identifies visitors. If its documentation is unclear, write “unknown” and avoid comparisons that require that information.

Use campaign names consistently

A simple naming convention such as 2026-10-newsletter-guide-pinterest is easier to compare than changing between “PIN,” “pinterest1,” and “OctoberSocial.” Record the exact tagged URL before publishing it. If you change campaign parameters halfway through a test, document the change rather than silently combining the reports.

Explain discrepancies before changing tactics

If clicks rise but sessions do not, check for broken URLs, slow loading, redirects that strip parameters, time-zone differences, and analytics consent settings. If submissions rise but confirmations do not, test the form, confirmation email, and provider settings. Do not jump directly to “bad traffic” without checking measurement.

Privacy-respecting visitors may block tracking. That is a limitation to report, not a reason to bypass their preferences.

Make one decision from the evidence

At the end of each week, write three sentences: what the sources recorded, what you cannot conclude, and what you will test next. For example: “Tagged sessions increased while confirmations stayed flat. We cannot tell whether the page copy or the audience caused that difference. Next week we will verify the mobile form and keep the traffic source constant.”

Avoid changing the headline, form, traffic source, and offer at the same time; you would not know which change mattered. A small, honest report supports better decisions than a dashboard full of unqualified numbers.
