Explain Sponsorship Clearly in an Email Newsletter

Disclose material connections beside the recommendation, while keeping the educational lesson useful without any purchase.

Home / Explain Sponsorship Clearly in an Email Newsletter

Educational guidance with fictional examples; not legal advice or a product endorsement.

Start with the actual relationship

An email recommendation can be educational and still involve a material connection. If you might earn a commission when a subscriber buys through your link, say so near the recommendation. If a company paid for placement, call it sponsored content rather than independent editorial advice. Free access, discounts, employment and other benefits can also matter. Readers should understand the connection before deciding whether to click, not after searching the footer.

The US FTC's Endorsement Guides FAQ discusses clear and conspicuous disclosure of material connections. The CRTC's CASL overview separately addresses consent, sender identification and unsubscribe requirements for Canadian commercial electronic messages. A sponsorship disclosure never substitutes for permission to send marketing email.

Choose accurate disclosure wording

Illustrative wording, not a universal legal formula
ArrangementPlain-language exampleVerify first
Commission-based referral“This is an affiliate link. I may earn a commission if you buy through it.”Current merchant eligibility and exact destination.
Paid newsletter placement“Sponsored placement: Company X paid to appear in this newsletter.”Sponsor name, payment and editorial conditions.
Free review access“The provider gave me access at no charge for this review.”What was supplied and whether testing really occurred.
No material connectionDo not invent a compensation relationship just to fill space.Any less-obvious connection that readers would reasonably care about.

Do not add “at no extra cost to you” unless that is verified. A tracking parameter in the link is not a disclosure. A generic “some links may be affiliate links” sentence at the bottom is easy to miss when a specific recommendation appears much earlier.

Put the notice where a skimming reader will see it

Suppose an email opens with a checklist and introduces a product halfway down. The disclosure belongs in that product section, before or alongside the recommendation. Use ordinary text with readable contrast so the statement survives image blocking and narrow screens. Do not rely on a badge graphic, hover text or an external policy page. If a reader must expand the email, scroll past unrelated material or open a new tab to learn about compensation, reconsider the placement.

A helpful sequence is: explain the problem, teach a free action, state the relationship, describe the resource accurately, offer a link only if warranted, and close with sender identity and a working unsubscribe mechanism. A reader should still benefit even if they never buy anything.

Worked example: a campaign measurement lesson

Problem: a beginner cannot tell whether tracked clicks turn into voluntary subscribers. Useful answer: record visits, completed forms and confirmed subscriptions in separate columns. In a fictional campaign there are 120 tracked visits, 9 completed forms and 7 confirmed subscriptions. The form-completion rate is 9 ÷ 120 = 7.5%; the confirmed-subscription rate is 7 ÷ 120 ≈ 5.8%. Neither figure establishes sales or future performance.

After teaching that calculation, an optional tools paragraph could say: “If you need a third-party tracker, compare event definitions, privacy controls and current terms. Affiliate disclosure: I may earn a commission if you purchase through the referral below.” This example deliberately contains no commercial link. The editor must first verify the actual tool, merchant terms, current offer and claim accuracy. A newsletter does not need a promotion in every issue.

Separate evidence from promotional language

Use a three-column ledger: claim, source checked and date checked. For a price, verify currency, billing interval and relevant conditions. For a feature, check a current official page or perform a documented test. For a comparison, say which options were compared and what remains unknown. For performance, distinguish illustrative arithmetic from real results. Avoid unsupported “best,” “guaranteed,” “unlimited” and earnings language. A disclosure does not make a false claim acceptable.

Review the actual sent email in mobile and plain-text views. A notice placed in an image may disappear when images are blocked. A button labeled only “Click here” hides its purpose; use a descriptive destination or action. For compensated outbound links in HTML you control, use rel="sponsored nofollow noopener noreferrer", but email providers may rewrite links and strip attributes, so verify delivered behavior. The disclosure sentence is still necessary.

Copyable pre-send sponsorship worksheet

  1. Reader task: What useful question does the email answer before promotion?
  2. Relationship: Who paid, supplied something of value or might pay a commission?
  3. Notice: Write one accurate, ordinary-language disclosure sentence.
  4. Placement: Can a mobile or plain-text reader see it beside the recommendation?
  5. Evidence: Which feature, price and comparison claims have dated verification?
  6. Destination: Does the current URL open the intended offer under its merchant terms?
  7. Permission: Does the mailing list have appropriate consent, sender identification and unsubscribe controls?
  8. Independent value: Would the reader still learn something without clicking?

Stop the promotion if the offer cannot be identified or the relationship cannot be described accurately. Keep a dated internal record of the decision. This is an editorial checklist, not a testimonial or legal determination.
