Map Search Intent Before Writing an Affiliate Guide

A search phrase is a clue about the reader's next task, not an instruction to repeat keywords. Map the task first, then decide whether a page deserves to exist.

Home / Map Search Intent Before Writing an Affiliate Guide
What you will learn: A practical method to distinguish learning, comparison and action-oriented searches, with a query-to-page worksheet and editorial decision rules.

Start with the job the reader is trying to finish

Imagine someone searches for “how to tell whether Pinterest visits subscribed to my newsletter.” They may want to understand attribution, set up a tracking link, or debug an analytics report. A generic “best traffic methods” sales page will not solve any of those jobs. A useful page would explain which numbers to compare, where uncertainty remains, and how to record a test.

Search intent is a working hypothesis about what a person hopes to accomplish. It is not a permanent label attached to a keyword. The same phrase can mean different things to different people, and search results change. The goal is to choose a narrow reader problem that your content can actually resolve.

Classify the next action, not just the wording

  • Learn: The reader wants an explanation, definition, or process. Example: “what is a referral visit?” A short illustrated explanation may be enough.
  • Compare: The reader has options and needs criteria. Example: “email newsletter versus social follow for updates.” Use a fair comparison and explain trade-offs.
  • Do: The reader wants instructions or a worksheet. Example: “set up a campaign naming convention.” Give exact steps and a completed example.
  • Troubleshoot: Something is not working. Example: “signup count differs from analytics conversions.” Give a diagnostic sequence rather than generic advice.
  • Navigate: The reader seeks a particular destination. Do not build a doorway page pretending to be an official service.

A query can contain several intents. Pick the dominant task for one page and link to a more detailed supporting page only when the reader genuinely needs it.

A five-column mapping worksheet

Create a sheet with query or question, reader situation, successful outcome, evidence needed, and best page format. Add one more column for the existing page that might already answer the question. That last check prevents near-duplicate articles.

For the Pinterest example, a completed row might say: “Beginner publisher using one newsletter form; can distinguish tagged Pinterest sessions from confirmed email subscriptions; needs a sample UTM link and form-provider count; troubleshooting guide; related existing page: UTM link builder.”

That row is much more actionable than “Pinterest traffic” as a topic. It also reveals the evidence you must provide: a definition of a visit, a definition of a subscription, and a warning that attribution systems can disagree.

Validate without copying search results

Review a few current results to understand common questions, but do not stitch their wording together. Look for gaps you can fill with an original example, an annotated screenshot you have permission to use, or a worked calculation. If you cannot add a distinctive example or reliable explanation, improve an existing page instead of creating another URL.

Write down what you observed separately from what you inferred. For example, “three pages include screenshots” is an observation; “all readers prefer screenshots” is an unsupported inference. Search results do not reveal what every visitor wants.

Write the first-screen answer

Draft two sentences that directly answer the task before outlining the article. For the example: “Use a tagged link to identify traffic from your Pinterest campaign, then compare that traffic with subscriptions recorded by your email provider. The two totals may differ because a visit, a form submission, and a confirmed subscriber are separate events.”

If the answer is vague, the topic may still be too broad. Rewrite it until a beginner could say what they will be able to do after reading.

Decide whether to publish

  • Publish a new guide if the task is distinct, the answer is reliable, and you can provide a real example or tool.
  • Extend an existing guide if the new question is a sub-step of the same task.
  • Combine overlapping drafts when both lead to the same action and evidence.
  • Skip a query if answering it would require unverifiable claims or a false promise.

Mini exercise: map three real questions

Collect three questions from a legitimate audience conversation or your own support notes, without recording private details. For each, write the reader's goal in one sentence, identify the evidence you need, and name the one page that should answer it. Compare the proposed URLs before publishing. If two pages have nearly identical answers, keep one stronger page.

This method makes content planning slower than automatic keyword expansion, but it gives every published page a clear reason to exist. Google's spam policies warn against scaled, low-value content created primarily to manipulate search rankings.
